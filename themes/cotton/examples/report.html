Not all cotton is equal.

The label tells you which one you're buying. "100% cotton" covers fibres that differ a lot in quality. This report explains the main types of cotton, why fibre length matters most, how fibre, yarn and weave together decide how sheets and t-shirts feel, and what to check on a label before you buy.

The types of cotton

Cotton is graded mainly by staple length, the length of a single fibre. Longer fibres spin into finer, stronger yarn with fewer loose ends, so the fabric is softer and smoother and pills less. Cotton comes from three groups of species, shown in colour throughout this section.

Organic, Better Cotton and recycled are not cotton types. They describe how the cotton was grown or sourced, not how long the fibre is. Organic cotton can be Upland or Pima.

From fibre to fabric

A good fibre can still become a poor fabric. These four steps decide how the fibre ends up feeling.

1

Combed, not carded

Combing removes short fibres and impurities, which leaves a smoother, stronger yarn. Carded-only yarn is cheaper and fuzzier.

Look for "combed"

2

Ring-spun yarn

Ring-spun and compact yarns are twisted tightly into a fine, soft thread. Open-end yarn is cheaper to make but feels rougher and drier.

Look for "ring-spun", "compact"

3

Weave or knit

Sheets are woven, as percale or sateen. T-shirts are knitted, usually as single jersey, which stretches and drapes.

Look for the weave name, gsm

4

Finishing

Mercerising adds lustre and strength. Pre-shrinking (or "sanforising") limits shrinkage to a few percent.

Look for "pre-shrunk", "mercerised"

How to read a label

Seven things to check, in order. Point at an item to find it on the sample label. The first three tell you most of what you need to know.

  1. 1

    Fibre content · low risk

    Must be listed as a percentage by law in the EU and US. "Cotton rich" or "cotton feel" means it's a blend, or not cotton at all.

    Check: a percentage for every fibre

  2. 2

    Cotton type · high risk

    The most common premium claim, and the one most often abused. Look-alike names such as "Egyptian-style" or "Pima feel" are marketing, not fibre.

    Check: Supima® trademark, Cotton Egypt Association logo, a named Giza variety

  3. 3

    Thread count · medium risk

    Threads per square inch. Some makers multiply the count by the number of plies to reach 800–1,200. The US FTC has called that misleading.

    Check: "single-ply", and a count of 200–600

  4. 4

    Yarn & construction · medium risk

    "Combed", "ring-spun" and "compact" show care in making the yarn. A missing weave name or weight is a small warning sign.

    Check: the weave name, or gsm for knits

  5. 5

    Certifications · medium risk

    GOTS covers organic fibre and processing. OEKO-TEX® 100 tests for harmful substances but isn't organic. Better Cotton supports better farming, but the product itself may not contain it.

    Check: a certificate or licence number you can look up

  6. 6

    Country of origin · for information

    Where the product was made (sewn or woven), not where the cotton grew. Egyptian cotton woven in India is normal.

    Check: don't read it as a quality signal

  7. 7

    Care instructions · low risk

    A hot-wash instruction on sheets is a good sign. "Wash before use" and "dry flat" often point to an unstable finish.

    Check: "pre-shrunk", and a 40–60 °C wash for sheets

1,000+ threads, low price

Almost always multi-ply yarns. Judge the fibre and the weave instead.

"Egyptian-quality"

Not a fibre claim. Real Egyptian cotton names itself and shows a logo.

"Cotton rich"

A blend, often with polyester. Look for the percentages.

"Heavyweight" at 130 gsm

The words and the numbers should agree. Heavyweight tees start around 200 gsm.

"Organic" with no certificate

GOTS or OCS backs an organic claim. OEKO-TEX® doesn't.

Staple
The length of a single cotton fibre.
ELS
Extra-long staple: fibres of about 35 mm (1⅜ in) or more.
Ply
The number of yarns twisted together into one thread.
Thread count
Horizontal plus vertical threads per square inch of woven fabric.
gsm
Grams per square metre: the fabric's weight.
Yarn count (Ne)
The fineness of a yarn. Higher numbers mean finer yarn.
Jersey
The single knit used for most t-shirts.
Mercerised
Treated with caustic soda for lustre, strength and better dye uptake.
