Cotton, decoded

"100% cotton" covers a wide range of quality. This report explains the main types of cotton, why fibre length matters most, and how fibre, yarn and weave together decide how bed sheets and t-shirts feel and wear. It ends with a checklist for reading labels.

The types of cotton

Cotton is graded mainly by staple length, the length of a single fibre. Longer fibres spin into finer, stronger yarn with fewer loose ends, so the fabric is softer, smoother and pills less. Select types below to compare them in the chart and table.

Organic, Better Cotton and recycled are not cotton types. They describe how the cotton was grown or sourced, not how long the fibre is. Organic cotton can be Upland or Pima.

From fibre to fabric

A good fibre can still become a poor fabric. These four steps decide how the fibre ends up feeling.

1 · Cleaning

Combed vs carded

Combing removes short fibres and impurities, which gives a smoother, stronger yarn. Carded-only yarn is cheaper and fuzzier.

Look for: "combed"

2 · Spinning

Ring-spun vs open-end

Ring-spun (and compact) yarn is twisted tightly into a fine, soft thread. Open-end yarn is cheaper to make but feels rougher and drier.

Look for: "ring-spun", "compact"

3 · Construction

Weave or knit

Sheets are woven (percale or sateen). T-shirts are knitted, usually as single jersey, which stretches and drapes.

Look for: the weave name, gsm

4 · Finishing

Mercerised, pre-shrunk

Mercerising adds lustre and strength. Pre-shrinking (or "sanforised") limits shrinkage to a few percent.

Look for: "pre-shrunk", "mercerised"

Checklist

What to look at when reading a label

Check these in order. The first three tell you most of what you need to know.

#Label saysWhat it really meansCheckRisk
1Fibre content (%) Must be listed by law in the EU and US. "Cotton rich" or "cotton feel" means it's a blend, or not cotton at all. A clear percentage for every fibreLow
2Cotton type (Egyptian, Pima, Supima) The most common premium claim, and the most often abused. Look-alike names such as "Egyptian-style" or "Pima feel" are marketing, not fibre. Supima® trademark, Cotton Egypt Association logo, a named Giza varietyHigh
3Thread count Threads per square inch. Some makers multiply the count by the number of plies to reach 800–1,200. The US FTC has called that misleading. "Single-ply", and a count of 200–600Medium
4Yarn & construction "Combed", "ring-spun" and "compact" show care in making the yarn. A missing weave name or weight is a small warning sign. Weave name (percale, sateen) or gsm for knitsMedium
5Certifications GOTS: organic fibre and processing. OEKO-TEX® 100: tested for harmful substances, not organic. Better Cotton: supports better farming practices, but the product itself may not contain it. A certificate or licence number you can look upMedium
6Country of origin Where the product was made (sewn or woven), not where the cotton was grown. Egyptian cotton woven in India is normal. Don't read it as a quality signalInfo
7Care instructions A hot-wash instruction on sheets is a good sign. "Wash before use" and "dry flat" often point to an unstable finish. "Pre-shrunk"; 40–60 °C wash for sheetsLow
Red flag

1,000+ thread count, low price

Almost always made with multi-ply yarns. Judge the fibre and weave instead.

Red flag

"Egyptian-quality"

Not a fibre claim. Real Egyptian cotton names itself and shows a logo.

Red flag

"Cotton rich"

A blend, often with polyester. Look for the percentages.

Red flag

"Heavyweight", 130 gsm

Labels and numbers should agree. Heavyweight tees start around 200 gsm.

Red flag

"Organic" without a certificate

GOTS or OCS certification backs an organic claim. OEKO-TEX® doesn't.
