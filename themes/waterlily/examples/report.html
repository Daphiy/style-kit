      What

      How many types are there

      Worldwide the genus has roughly 50–70 species, depending on which classification you follow. Seven native species have US records. One of them, the fragrant white waterlily, accounts for 9 in 10 of all records.

      Status

      Ecological status at a glance

      A qualitative summary. Check the formal conservation ranks per state before using these for any decision.

      Widespread

      Fragrant white waterlily

      Common in ponds, lakes and slow rivers across the East, the Great Lakes states and the Gulf Coast, and recorded in every region of the country. It is generally regarded as secure.

      Out of place

      Spreading beyond its native range

      In the Pacific Northwest and California the fragrant white waterlily is introduced and can form dense mats that shade out native plants. Washington lists it as a noxious weed. The yellow banana waterlily, native to Florida and the Gulf, also turns up in California (49 records).

      Rare

      Northern dwarfs

      Leiberg’s and pygmy waterlilies are small, cold-water species. They have only a few hundred records, scattered across Minnesota, Maine, Montana, Idaho and Alaska, at the southern edge of a mostly boreal range. Several states treat them as rare.

      Local

      Subtropical specialists

      The blue, Jameson’s and dotleaf waterlilies grow only in the warm South: Texas, Florida and Louisiana. 72 of the 74 Jameson’s records are from Florida, and the dotleaf has just 26 US records in total.

      How to read these numbers

      • Records are not population counts. A record is one dated sighting or specimen. State totals reflect survey effort, lake density and how popular community science apps are locally, as well as how common the plants are.
      • The rise since 2015 is mostly more observers. Photo observations grew more than tenfold in a decade, while museum collecting fell. The data can’t tell whether waterlily populations themselves are growing or shrinking.
      • Recent specimen counts lag. Museums take years to digitise collections, so the last few years of specimens are likely undercounted.
      • Status is a summary, not a ranking. For formal ranks, see NatureServe Explorer and each state’s natural heritage program.
